# Grammar Principles

## Main Principle

Function first, form second.

A structure entering a sentence must perform a function. Only after the function is clear should you name its form.

## Sentence Skeleton

Find finite predicates first. Then identify the main clause and basic pattern:

- SV: subject + intransitive verb
- SVO: subject + transitive verb + object
- SVC: subject + linking verb + complement / predicative
- SVOO: subject + verb + indirect object + direct object
- SVOC: subject + verb + object + object complement

Treat these five as the Larry core skeletons, not an exhaustive list of every surface pattern in real prose. Add a secondary label when needed:

- SVA: a verb plus a selected place, direction, or similar element
- SVOA: a transitive verb whose current sense also selects a place or direction, as in `put`
- existential `there + be`
- dummy/formal `it` with an extraposed clause
- passive surface order, while retaining the underlying action relation
- coordination, where each coordinated clause has its own skeleton

Never force these structures into the five-pattern list by deleting a selected element or treating multiple finite predicates as one skeleton.

## Clause Types

Classify a clause by its overall function in the larger sentence:

- noun clause: occupies a noun position
- adjective clause / relative clause: modifies a noun or noun phrase
- adverbial clause: modifies a verb, adjective, adverb, clause, or whole sentence by logic such as time, place, reason, condition, concession, purpose, result, manner, comparison

Do not confuse a clause’s internal subject/predicate/object with the clause’s overall function.

Do not define every clause as `connector + complete simple sentence`. Locate its finite predicate and dependency first. A clause introducer may be omitted; a wh/relative item may itself fill an internal role; a relative clause may contain a gap linked to its antecedent.

## Clause Introducers

A connector does not always fill a sentence component.

- In noun clauses, `what`, `who`, `which`, etc. often fill an internal role; `that` often only marks connection.
- In relative clauses, relative pronouns/adverbs usually connect to the antecedent and fill an internal role or relation.
- In adverbial clauses, conjunctions such as because, if, although, when usually mark logic and do not serve as ordinary noun-like components.

## Non-Finite Forms

A non-finite form has verb meaning but lacks finite predicate status.

Check:

1. form: to do / doing / done / having done / being done
2. logical subject
3. active or passive relation
4. sentence function: noun, adjective, adverb, complement, or supplement
5. whether it can be restored to a clause

## Tense, Aspect, And Situation Type

Keep these layers separate:

1. tense / time location: where the finite predicate locates the situation
2. grammatical aspect: simple, progressive, perfect, or perfect progressive viewpoint
3. situation type: the state, activity, accomplishment, or achievement expressed by the complete predicate in context
4. discourse meaning: habit, temporary situation, background, plan, politeness, or psychological distance

Do not assign a permanent situation type to an isolated verb. Objects, quantity, negation, grammatical aspect, and context can change the situation described. Use `有终点过程 (accomplishment)` in Chinese so it is not confused with `完成体 (perfect)`.

Progressive and perfect-progressive forms focus a process and do not by themselves assert whether a lexical endpoint was eventually reached. `since` marks the left boundary of an interval, not necessarily a point-like event.

## Complement Test

For suspected complements, use three checks:

```text
subject/object is or becomes complement
subject/object does or undergoes the non-finite action
```

1. A coherent mini-clause supports a predicative relation, but does not by itself prove that the complement is obligatory.
2. For infinitival or participial complements, identify the logical subject instead of forcing an `A is B` paraphrase.
3. Delete the candidate only to distinguish an obligatory selected complement from an optional secondary predicate. A removable secondary predicate is not automatically an adverbial.
4. If the candidate only describes time, place, reason, manner, condition, or background of the action and does not predicate something of the subject/object, it is probably adverbial.

## Prepositional Phrases

A prepositional phrase contains a preposition plus a noun-function object. The whole phrase usually functions adjectivally or adverbially.

Always identify:

1. the preposition
2. the preposition object
3. what the whole phrase modifies or completes

For this project's teaching display, route the whole PP first:

- yellow adjectival route when it modifies or restricts a noun
- green adverbial route when it relates to a verb, adjective, adverb, or clause

Then add a secondary syntactic label when necessary, such as predicative/subject complement or selected locative complement. A green route does not mean the PP is always optional.

## Translation Rule

Translation is the final step, not the first step. Translate after the skeleton and important structures are judged.
